The SBOM and licence side of software composition analysis is about the inventory and the legal posture: producing a machine-readable list of every component in an application, and knowing which open-source licences that list contains and what obligations they carry. This reference covers SBOM formats, generation and consumption, the regulatory drivers, and the open-source licence classes and the policy that gates them. The dependency-tree and vulnerability side lives in `dependency-and-vulnerability.md`. The container-IMAGE SBOM (image layers, OS packages, SLSA provenance, signing, admission) is out of scope here and belongs to `container-security`; this reference is the APPLICATION SBOM, describing the libraries the application declared.

## What an SBOM is

A software bill of materials (SBOM) is a machine-readable inventory of all the components that make up a piece of software: every dependency, its version, and metadata such as its supplier, its licence, and often its cryptographic hash. It is the manifest of what is actually inside the artefact.

The SBOM is not just a compliance deliverable; it is the working artefact the rest of SCA reads from. Vulnerability tracking consumes it to know which components to match against advisory databases, and licence policy consumes it to know which licences are present. Generating it once in the build and keeping it current means the tree does not have to be re-derived on every check. The application SBOM describes the application-and-library scope; an image SBOM describing OS packages and layers is a different document with a different scope, even when the same tool and format produce both.

## SBOM formats

Three formats dominate, two of them primary:

| Format | Steward | Character |
|---|---|---|
| CycloneDX | OWASP | Security-focused, widely supported in AppSec tooling, carries vulnerability and VEX data well |
| SPDX (Software Package Data Exchange) | Linux Foundation | ISO/IEC 5962 standard, strong on licensing metadata, NIST-recognised |
| SWID (Software Identification) tags | ISO/IEC 19770-2 | Used in enterprise and government asset-management contexts |

CycloneDX and SPDX are the two an application SBOM is almost always produced in. CycloneDX leans security-first and pairs naturally with vulnerability and VEX ("vulnerability exploitability exchange") statements; SPDX leans licensing-and-provenance-first and is the more common choice where the driver is legal or supply-chain transparency. Both are well supported by the common generators, and many tools can emit either. SWID is more niche, seen where an existing software asset-management regime already uses it.

## Generating and consuming an SBOM

An SBOM is generated by a tool that reads the resolved dependency tree (the lockfile and manifest) and emits it in a standard format. It should be produced in the build, so it reflects exactly what shipped, and regenerated on every build so it never goes stale. Illustrative generators, by ecosystem:

```bash
# CycloneDX for npm
npx @cyclonedx/cyclonedx-npm --output-file sbom.json

# CycloneDX for Maven
mvn org.cyclonedx:cyclonedx-maven-plugin:makeBom

# CycloneDX for Python
pip install cyclonedx-bom
cyclonedx-bom -r -o sbom.xml

# SPDX for a filesystem or package tree via syft
syft dir:. -o spdx-json > sbom.spdx.json
```

Consuming an SBOM is the other half. A downstream tool reads the document to run vulnerability matching against it, to check licences against policy, or to answer a "what is affected by CVE-X" question across a fleet without rescanning each artefact. A customer or regulator may also require the SBOM as a deliverable in its own right. The failure mode to avoid is generating an SBOM once for an audit and never again: an SBOM is only as useful as it is current, and a stale one misrepresents what actually ships.

## Why SBOMs are required

The demand for SBOMs is increasingly driven by regulation and by customer procurement, not only by internal good practice:

- **US Executive Order 14028 (2021)** directed federal agencies to require SBOMs from software vendors, which pushed the requirement down through the government supply chain.
- **EU Cyber Resilience Act** brings SBOM-style obligations for products with digital elements sold into the EU.
- **Medical device software** falls under FDA guidance that expects an SBOM for premarket submissions.
- **Financial services and critical infrastructure** face rising regulatory and contractual expectations to produce and maintain SBOMs.

The practical point for SCA is to know which driver is behind a given SBOM request, because it determines the format, the required fields, and how often the document must be refreshed and delivered.

## Open-source licence classes

Every open-source component ships under a licence, and the licences differ sharply in the obligations they impose. SCA classifies them so a policy can act on them:

- **Permissive** (MIT, BSD 2-Clause and 3-Clause, Apache 2.0, ISC). Generally safe for commercial and proprietary use. The main obligation is attribution: preserve the copyright and licence notice. Apache 2.0 adds an explicit patent grant.
- **Weak copyleft** (LGPL v2 and v3, Mozilla MPL 2.0). File-level or component-level copyleft. LGPL typically permits dynamic linking without triggering copyleft on the larger work, while static linking may trigger it. MPL 2.0 requires that modifications to the licensed files themselves stay open, but does not reach the rest of the application.
- **Strong copyleft** (GPL v2, GPL v3). Distributing software that incorporates GPL code requires releasing the combined work's source under the GPL. This is a serious obligation for a proprietary product that is distributed.
- **Network copyleft** (AGPL v3). Extends the trigger to use over a network: offering AGPL-covered functionality to users over a network counts as distribution, so a SaaS that incorporates AGPL code can be obliged to release its source. This is the highest-risk class for a hosted or SaaS product.
- **Commercially restricted** (CC BY-NC, and source-available licences such as BUSL and SSPL). These are not classic open source and may restrict commercial use, competing services, or hosting outright. They need reading on their own terms rather than fitting a copyleft pattern.

The obligation escalates down the list: attribution, then file-level openness, then distribution-level source release, then network-use source release. The classification is what lets a policy treat "safe", "review", and "blocked" differently instead of reacting to each licence individually.

## Licence policy and CI gating

A licence policy turns the classification into an automated control with three lists:

1. **Approved.** Licences cleared for use without review, typically the permissive set (MIT, Apache 2.0, BSD, ISC). Components under these pass silently.
2. **Requires review.** Licences that need a human, usually legal, to decide case by case: LGPL, MPL, and the source-available and commercially restricted licences whose acceptability depends on how the component is used and distributed.
3. **Blocked.** Licences incompatible with the product's distribution model, typically GPL and AGPL for a proprietary or SaaS product. A component under a blocked licence fails the build.

The SCA tool enforces the policy by reading each component's licence from the SBOM and failing CI when a blocked licence appears, before the dependency ships. Catching it at build time is the whole point: the alternative is discovering an AGPL dependency during a customer's or acquirer's legal due-diligence, when it is far more expensive to remove. As with vulnerabilities, the earlier the gate fires the cheaper the fix, and a build-time licence gate is the cheapest place to catch a licence the organisation cannot accept.
